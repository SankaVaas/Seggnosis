# Empirical validation results

Generated by `python benchmarks/validate_library.py`. This trains a real
(small) model on a synthetic-but-structured segmentation task -- 32x32
images, 3 overlapping shape classes + background, blurred edges, and
*randomized* labels in shape-overlap regions (irreducible label noise, like
real annotator disagreement) -- then checks seggnosis's output against
ground truth. Unlike the unit test suite, this is checking whether the
library's numbers are actually *meaningful*, not just correctly shaped.

Raw output: `results_output.txt`. Re-run any time with
`python benchmarks/validate_library.py` from inside `benchmarks/`.

## 1. Uncertainty quality -- validated

Held-out accuracy by uncertainty quartile (lowest -> highest uncertainty):

| method | Q1 | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 | corr(uncertainty, correctness) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mc_dropout | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.994 | 0.868 | -0.385 |
| tta | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.972 | 0.888 | -0.092 |
| ensemble | 1.000 | 0.999 | 0.985 | 0.879 | -0.231 |

All three methods: accuracy drops sharply in the highest-uncertainty
quartile, and the uncertainty/correctness correlation is negative as
expected. This is the core claim of the library and it holds up on a model
that wasn't trained to make this result easy to get (it was trained to
convergence on the task; the errors it still makes are concentrated in the
genuinely ambiguous overlap regions, and uncertainty finds them).

## 1b. Entropy vs. mutual information (aleatoric vs. epistemic) -- inconclusive

The idea: pixels in shape-overlap regions have literally unpredictable
labels by construction, so total uncertainty (entropy) should fire there,
while epistemic-only uncertainty (mutual information, via ensemble
disagreement) *shouldn't* necessarily be elevated there -- the models
aren't ignorant, the label is just unresolvable.

The result didn't show a clean separation (MI's ratio came out slightly
*higher* than entropy's, not lower). On reflection the test design is
weak: it selects "ambiguous" pixels using entropy's own threshold, which
biases the comparison, and both ensemble members were trained on the same
randomized-overlap-label regime, so they can genuinely disagree with each
other on a specific overlap pixel too -- conflating epistemic and aleatoric
uncertainty in the test setup, not just in the measurement. **This check
needs a better-isolated setup to be trustworthy; don't treat the current
number as confirming or refuting anything about entropy vs. MI.**

## 2. OOD detection -- mechanism confirmed, but the test was too easy to be discriminating

| comparison | AUROC |
|---|---|
| in-distribution vs. far-OOD (pure noise) | 1.000 |
| in-distribution vs. near-OOD (heavy sensor noise) | 1.000 |
| in-distribution vs. near-OOD (color-shifted) | 1.000 |

The detector isn't broken (a broken one would score ~0.5, not 1.0 across
the board). But a small, well-converged model's feature space is tight
enough that *any* distribution shift was trivially obvious here -- this
doesn't tell us how the detector performs on a harder near-OOD case (e.g. a
genuinely similar-looking but subtly different class, or a real dataset
with a real domain shift). Treat this as "the mechanism works," not "the
detector is this good in general."

## 3. Calibration -- the benchmark didn't exercise this feature meaningfully

- ECE before scaling: 0.0054
- ECE after scaling: 0.0072 (learned temperature: 1.097, i.e. almost no-op)
- Did not improve.

This small model trained to convergence on a simple task was already close
to calibrated, so there was nothing for temperature scaling to fix -- the
slight ECE increase is noise around an already-tiny number, not evidence
the method is broken. **To actually validate temperature scaling we need a
model/task combination that's genuinely overconfident** (larger model
relative to task complexity, shorter/earlier-stopped training, or
evaluation under distribution shift, which is when overconfidence typically
shows up in practice). Worth a follow-up benchmark.

## 4. Conformal prediction -- coverage holds, but reveals a real limitation

| alpha | target coverage | empirical coverage | avg. prediction-set size |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 90% | 98.3% | 1.00 / 4 |
| 0.2 | 80% | 98.3% | 1.00 / 4 |

Coverage is valid (the guarantee is "at least" the target, and both targets
are comfortably met). But the average prediction-set size staying at
*exactly* 1.00 -- even on the genuinely-ambiguous overlap pixels -- means
the model is confidently (if sometimes wrongly) picking a single class
there rather than expressing uncertainty between two plausible ones. That's
a known failure mode of neural nets trained with standard cross-entropy on
noisy labels: they tend to memorize a confident answer rather than output
calibrated ~50/50 probabilities on genuinely ambiguous inputs.

This points to a real, specific upgrade opportunity for seggnosis's
conformal method: the current implementation is the simple ("LAC") variant
-- a single global probability threshold. **Adaptive variants (APS /
RAPS)** are specifically designed to produce prediction-set sizes that
track per-pixel difficulty better, rather than inheriting whatever
confidence pattern the underlying model happens to produce. Worth
considering as the next conformal-prediction improvement.

## Honest summary

- **Uncertainty quantification (mc_dropout/tta/ensemble): genuinely
  validated.** This is the strongest result here.
- **OOD detection: mechanism confirmed, not yet stress-tested** on a
  harder near-OOD case or real data.
- **Calibration: untested in any discriminating way** by this benchmark --
  needs a deliberately overconfident model to be a real test.
- **Conformal prediction: coverage guarantee holds**, and this benchmark
  surfaced a legitimate, specific limitation (LAC vs. APS/RAPS) rather than
  just passing trivially.
